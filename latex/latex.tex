\begin{abstract}
Segmenting small, thin, and highly shape-variant anatomical structures remains challenging, since both square-kernel convolutions and global-context models are ill-suited to elongated organ geometries. To address these shortcomings, we present MultiStripUNet, an anatomy-aware U-shaped network. It replaces square kernels with the Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC) module, which provides an implicit structural prior for anisotropic anatomy, letting organs with distinct aspect ratios be matched by an appropriate scale. The MSC, together with lightweight channel and spatial attention, forms the Multi-Strip Convolution Block (MSCB) to build both the encoder and the decoder; the Multi-Strip Convolution Gate (MSCG) further replaces the square-kernel attention gates on skip connections to make the attention mask itself anisotropy-aware. On the Synapse dataset, MultiStripUNet achieves 85.86\% mean Dice and 13.36\,mm HD95, surpassing the strongest baseline by +0.74 DSC and +0.15\,mm HD95, with the largest gains on the small organs Aorta (+1.24), Gallbladder (+1.01), and Pancreas (+2.09); on ACDC it attains 92.82\% mean Dice, +0.19 over the best baseline, with only 26.21M parameters. Ablations show that strip convolutions are the dominant performance driver and that the spacing between strip kernel sizes governs the accuracy--efficiency trade-off.

\keywords{Medical Image Segmentation \and Strip Convolution \and Anatomy-Aware Representation Learning.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Medical image segmentation is central to computer-assisted diagnosis, and deep networks have driven remarkable progress in this area over the past decade \cite{he2016resnet,lecun2015deep}: fully convolutional U-Net-style models \cite{isensee2021nnu,oktay2018attention,ronneberger2015unet,zhou2018unet++}, Transformer-based models \cite{cao2021swinunet,chen2021transunet,dosovitskiy2020vit,hatamizadeh2022unetr,vaswani2017attention}, and state-space models such as Mamba and its visual variants \cite{gu2023mamba,liu2024swinumamba,liu2024vmamba,ma2024umamba,ruan2024vmunet,zhu2024vim}. Despite this sustained progress, one obstacle has proven stubborn: the accurate segmentation of \emph{small, thin, and highly shape-variant} anatomical structures.

Such structures (the aorta, gallbladder, and pancreas in abdominal CT, or the right ventricle in cardiac MRI) are slender, elongated, and strongly curved, occupying only a small fraction of the image. Their strong anisotropy mismatches the isotropic square kernels of standard convolutions, which must grow large to cover elongated organs and thereby aggregate background context; and global-context models, whether attention- or state-space-based, do not explicitly respect the directional, boundary-like geometry of these structures, leaving their fine boundaries to be recovered by the decoder. Shallow skip connections further aggravate the problem, as they carry abundant background noise, which is particularly detrimental to tiny, slender organs, and gating mechanisms built on standard square convolutions cannot precisely conform to elongated organ boundaries.

These problems share a common cause: the architecture is not shaped by the anatomy it segments. We therefore design MultiStripUNet around the geometry of organs, addressing each issue in turn. First, square kernels extract elongated features poorly. We replace them with strip convolutions and, by combining multiple kernel sizes, build the Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC) module, which matches organs from the finely elongated pancreas to the broadly extended aorta at appropriate scales while covering far less background than square kernels of the same extent, so the surrounding noise is suppressed. Second, anisotropic modeling should not be confined to one component, since square-kernel encoder and decoder stages leave slender structures to be recovered by coarse features. We therefore assemble the MSC with lightweight channel and spatial attention into the Multi-Strip Convolution Block (MSCB) and use it as the building block of both the encoder and the decoder. Third, the attention gate of Attention U-Net \cite{oktay2018attention} is built from square kernels and cannot align with elongated organ boundaries; we instead build the gate from strip convolutions, so that the resulting Multi-Strip Convolution Gate (MSCG) reweights skip-connection features with an anisotropy-aware mask and preserves fine structural details along the encoder--decoder pathway.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We identify the geometric mismatch between square kernels and elongated anatomical structures as a main obstacle, and propose the Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC) module, which uses multiple strip-kernel pairs of different sizes as an implicit structural prior for anisotropic anatomy; systematic ablations, including strip-vs-square comparisons, show that both the kernel shape and the scale spacing matter.
\item We build MultiStripUNet from MSCBs in both the encoder and the decoder, with MSCG-gated skip connections, using only 26.21M parameters.
\item MultiStripUNet achieves 85.86\% mean DSC / 13.36\,mm HD95 on Synapse and 92.82\% mean DSC on ACDC, with the largest gains on the small target organs (Aorta, Gallbladder, Pancreas).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Works}

\textbf{Convolutional networks for medical segmentation.} U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} established the encoder--decoder paradigm with skip connections; V-Net \cite{milletari2016v}, Attention U-Net \cite{oktay2018attention}, UNet++ \cite{zhou2018unet++}, and nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnu} extended it toward volumetric inputs, attention gating, and self-configuration. Large-kernel designs (ConvNeXt \cite{liu2022convnext}, RepLKNet \cite{ding2022scaling}) enlarge receptive fields at the price of increased computation, an issue that strip-shaped kernels mitigate by design.

\textbf{Transformer-based methods.} Vision Transformer \cite{dosovitskiy2020vit} and Swin Transformer \cite{liu2021swin} brought self-attention to vision, and medical models such as TransUNet \cite{chen2021transunet}, Swin-UNet \cite{cao2021swinunet}, UNETR \cite{hatamizadeh2022unetr}, MISSFormer \cite{huang2022missformer}, and TransCASCADE \cite{rahman2023transcascade} followed. These methods excel at global context but incur quadratic attention cost, often rely on pre-trained weights, and remain limited in boundary precision on small structures.

\textbf{Mamba-based methods.} State-space models \cite{fu2022hungry,gu2020hippo,gu2021efficiently} and the selective state-space model Mamba \cite{gu2023mamba} provide linear-complexity global modeling; visual adaptations \cite{liu2024vmamba,zhu2024vim} led to medical networks such as VM-UNet \cite{ruan2024vmunet}, U-Mamba \cite{ma2024umamba}, Swin-UMamba \cite{liu2024swinumamba}, ACM-UNet \cite{acmunet2025}, and NexMamba \cite{nexmamba2026}. MambaOut \cite{yu2024mambaout} has questioned whether the selective-scan mechanism is indispensable, and our results echo this view: MultiStripUNet reaches competitive accuracy without any state-space backbone.

\textbf{Strip-shaped operators.} Strip pooling \cite{hou2020strip} showed that elongated pooling regions capture long-range context, and SLaK \cite{liu2023slak} decomposed large square kernels into strip pairs for efficiency; Active Strip Convolution \cite{chu2021active} adapted strip kernels to medical images. These works, however, use a single strip kernel size or a fixed decomposition attached to a standard backbone, without a systematic study of how strip kernels should be configured for anatomy. MultiStripUNet is instead driven by the geometry of anatomical structures: it generalizes strip convolution to multiple kernel sizes, places it in both the encoder and the decoder, and shows by ablation that the strip shape and its scale spacing are the decisive design factors.

\section{Methodology}

This section first introduces the overall architecture of MultiStripUNet, followed by detailed descriptions of each constituent module.

\subsection{MultiStripUNet}

Figure~\ref{figure1}(a) presents the overall architecture of MultiStripUNet, which follows the U-shaped encoder--decoder design \cite{ronneberger2015unet}. A Patch Embedding layer tokenizes the input into $4\times4$ patches, producing features of size $\frac{H}{4}\times\frac{W}{4}\times C$ for an $H\times W$ input. The encoder comprises four MSCB-based stages; between consecutive stages, a Patch Merging layer halves the spatial resolution and doubles the channels, yielding hierarchical features $x_1$ to $x_4$ at $\frac{H}{4}$ to $\frac{H}{32}$ resolution with $C$ to $8C$ channels. The decoder is symmetric, with four MSCB-based stages each preceded by a Patch Expanding layer that upsamples and halves the channels. The three shallower skips $x_1$--$x_3$ are reweighted by MSCG gates before fusion into the decoder, while the deepest features $x_4$ are fed directly to the bottom decoder stage. A Segmentation Head (SH), a $1\times1$ convolution, projects the topmost decoder features to the segmentation map.

\subsection{Multi-Strip Convolution Gate (MSCG)}

The Multi-Strip Convolution Gate (MSCG) addresses a weakness of plain skip connections: shallow encoder features carry abundant background noise, which is especially detrimental to tiny, slender organs. Classical attention gates \cite{oktay2018attention} generate the attention map with square convolutions, but such isotropic kernels cannot conform to elongated organ boundaries. We therefore embed the MSC module directly into the gate, giving the gate itself anisotropic perception. As shown in Figure~\ref{figure1}(c), the decoder feature $g$ and the skip feature $x$ are both processed by $MSC(\cdot)$, merged by element-wise addition, and passed through GELU, a $1\times1$ convolution ($Cnv_{1\times1}(\cdot)$), batch normalization ($BN(\cdot)$), and a sigmoid $\sigma(\cdot)$ to yield single-channel attention coefficients, with which the original skip features $x$ are multiplied. The mask therefore marks slender structures precisely while filtering out the surrounding isotropic noise.

\subsection{Multi-Strip Convolution Block (MSCB)}

The Multi-Strip Convolution Block (MSCB) is the core building block of both the encoder and the decoder (Figure~\ref{figure1}(b)). Before the strip pathway refines the features, two lightweight attention modules re-calibrate them along complementary dimensions. The Channel Attention (CA) block emphasizes which channels encode task-relevant anatomy, while the Spatial Attention (SA) block points to where the salient regions are located. The re-calibrated features then enter the Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC), which captures anisotropic structures at multiple scales. Applying CA, SA, and MSC in this order lets the strip pathway work on already re-calibrated features, so the anisotropic modeling benefits the most at a minimal extra cost.

\textbf{Channel Attention Block (CA)}\cite{woo2018cbam}: As shown in Figure~\ref{figure1}(e), the CA block focuses on identifying which channels carry task-relevant anatomical information. Given an input feature map $x$, the module first aggregates the spatial dimension via adaptive max pooling ($AMP(\cdot)$) and adaptive average pooling ($AAP(\cdot)$) \cite{he2014spatial}, producing two channel descriptors that capture the most salient and the average response of each channel. Each descriptor is then compressed by a point-wise convolution ($PWC_{C/r}(\cdot)$, reduction ratio $r=16$) \cite{lin2013network}, activated by ReLU, and restored by $PWC_C(\cdot)$. The two descriptors are fused by element-wise addition, and a sigmoid generates the channel attention weights, which re-scale $x$ through element-wise multiplication.

\textbf{Spatial Attention Block (SA)}\cite{woo2018cbam}: As illustrated in Figure~\ref{figure1}(f), the SA block focuses on identifying where the salient anatomical features are located. Given an input feature map $x$, the module first applies max pooling and average pooling along the channel axis. This operation aggregates the input into two 2D spatial descriptors, efficiently highlighting the most prominent and average feature responses across all channels. These descriptors are then concatenated and processed by a large-kernel ($7\times7$, padding 3) convolution to capture a broad spatial context. Finally, a sigmoid activation generates a spatial attention mask, which is applied to the original input $x$ via element-wise multiplication to spatially recalibrate the features.

\subsection{Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC)}

The Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC) module \cite{chu2021active,hou2020strip,liu2023slak} forms the core of the MSCB. Isotropic square kernels naturally fail on elongated organs, and because this anisotropy varies across anatomy, with a finely elongated pancreas requiring a different receptive scale than a broadly extended aorta, MSC employs multiple parallel strip pairs of distinct lengths $N_i$ (we set $\{17, 21\}$ in our experiments). As outlined in Figure~\ref{figure1}(d), the input first passes through layer normalization, a fully connected layer, and GELU \cite{hendrycks2016gelu}, yielding a projected base feature $x'$ that enters the core Strip Module (Figure~\ref{figure1}(g)). There, a $5\times5$ depthwise convolution anchors the local spatial continuity; the feature then splits into decoupled horizontal ($1\times N_i$) and vertical ($N_i\times1$) branches that capture multi-scale anisotropic dependencies without sacrificing fine local detail. Once aggregated by point-wise convolution, this strip descriptor modulates $x'$ by element-wise multiplication; the gated result, combined with the input through a residual connection, is refined by a Feed-Forward Network (FFN).

\subsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss}

We employ a hybrid loss combining Soft Dice Loss and Focal Loss, whose roles are complementary. Soft Dice Loss provides a region-level objective that maximizes the spatial overlap between the predicted masks and the ground truth, while Focal Loss operates per-pixel: via a modulating factor $(1-p_t)^\gamma$ it down-weights easy examples, so that learning concentrates on the hard-to-classify pixels near anatomical boundaries. Their combination therefore yields both global structural consistency and local boundary sharpness, which a single term cannot achieve alone.

The objective function is formulated as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dice}
  L_{Dice} = 1 - \frac{2\sum_{i} p_i g_i + \epsilon}{\sum_{i} p_i + \sum_{i} g_i + \epsilon}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:focal}
  L_{Focal} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} \alpha (1 - p_{t,i})^{\gamma} \log(p_{t,i})
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:total}
  L_{total} = \lambda_{dice} L_{Dice} + \lambda_{focal} L_{Focal}
\end{equation}

where $p_i$ and $g_i$ denote the predicted probability and the ground-truth label of pixel $i$, $p_{t,i} = p_i$ if $y_i = 1$ and $1 - p_i$ otherwise, $N$ is the number of pixels, and $\lambda_{dice}$ and $\lambda_{focal}$ are the weighting coefficients of each component. In our experiments, we empirically set $\lambda_{dice} = 1$ and $\lambda_{focal} = 1$ to maintain a balanced gradient flow throughout the backpropagation process, ensuring both global structural consistency and local boundary sharpness.

\section{Experiments}

This section presents the details of implementation followed by a comparative analysis of our MultiStripUNet against competing methods, including the performance of the complete model on the datasets and the details of the ablation study.

\subsection{Implementation details}\label{sec:impl}

To evaluate the proposed MultiStripUNet, comprehensive experiments are conducted on two widely adopted public benchmarks: the Synapse multi-organ segmentation dataset \cite{landman2015miccai} and the Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC) dataset \cite{bernard2018deep}.

\textbf{Datasets.} The Synapse dataset comprises 30 abdominal CT scans with 13 abdominal organs annotated. Following the standard protocol \cite{cao2021swinunet,chen2021transunet}, we adopt the official split of 18 training cases and 12 testing cases. The ACDC dataset contains 100 cardiac MRI scans from 100 patients, each with annotations for the right ventricle (RV), left ventricle (LV), and myocardium (Myo). We follow the standard split of 70 training cases, 10 validation cases, and 20 testing cases \cite{bernard2018deep}. Patient-level separation is strictly enforced to prevent data leakage between training and test sets.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} All images are uniformly resized to a spatial resolution of $224 \times 224$, and intensity values are normalized to zero mean and unit variance. To mitigate the risk of overfitting and enhance generalization, standard data augmentation techniques including random flipping and random rotation are employed.

\textbf{Training Protocol.} MultiStripUNet is trained from scratch without pre-trained weights. We set the batch size to 32 and employ the AdamW optimizer \cite{loshchilov2017decoupled} with an initial learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-5}$ and weight decay of $1 \times 10^{-3}$. The learning rate is scheduled by cosine annealing \cite{loshchilov2016sgdr} with a linear warmup over the first 50 epochs followed by a cosine decay; the entire training process spans 300 epochs. For the hybrid loss function, the Focal Loss parameters are set to $\alpha = 0.5$ and $\gamma = 0.5$, and the Dice Loss uses a smoothing factor of $\epsilon = 1 \times 10^{-3}$.

\textbf{Evaluation.} For quantitative assessment, two primary metrics are used: the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) to measure region-based overlap \cite{milletari2016v}, and the 95\% Hausdorff Distance (HD95) to evaluate boundary-based accuracy \cite{huttenlocher1993comparing}.

All experiments are implemented using the PyTorch framework and executed on a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. For reliable comparison, every model is trained five times with different random seeds and the mean results are reported; the standard deviations of MultiStripUNet are $\pm 0.20$ on Synapse and $\pm 0.30$ on ACDC.

\subsection{Experimental Results}

We compare MultiStripUNet with competitive methods from three architectural families: convolutional networks (U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet}, Attention U-Net \cite{oktay2018attention}, nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnu}, GoLoCo-Net \cite{goloco2026}), Transformer-based models (TransUNet \cite{chen2021transunet}, Swin-UNet \cite{cao2021swinunet}, PolypPVT \cite{dong2021polyp}, MSA$^2$-Net \cite{msa2net2026}, PVT-EMCAD-B2 \cite{emcad2024}), and Mamba-based models (MSM-UNet \cite{msmunet2025}, ACM-UNet \cite{acmunet2025}, NexMamba \cite{nexmamba2026}). For a fair comparison, all baselines are trained and evaluated under the same protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

\subsubsection{Results on Synapse Multi-Organ Segmentation}

Results on Synapse are presented in Table~\ref{table1}. MultiStripUNet achieves the best mean DSC of 85.86\% and HD95 of 13.36\,mm, outperforming the strongest baselines NexMamba \cite{nexmamba2026} (85.32\%/13.51\,mm) and ACM-UNet \cite{acmunet2025} (84.90\%/13.92\,mm). The advantages are most pronounced on the small, thin, and highly shape-variant organs: Aorta 89.51\% (vs. 88.27\%), Gallbladder 75.83\% (vs. 74.82\%), and Pancreas 75.21\% (vs. 73.12\%, +2.09 over the strongest baseline), and both kidneys are also best (90.31\%/85.47\%). We attribute these gains to the multi-scale strip convolutions of the MSCB, which match the elongated geometry of these structures, and to the MSCG, which preserves their fine details along the skip connections.

Figure~\ref{figure2} presents qualitative comparisons on two representative cases. In Case04-96, the slender, low-contrast pancreas is incompletely or fragmentarily segmented by most competing methods, whereas MultiStripUNet delineates the entire organ; in Case08-108, the closely spaced left and right kidneys are over- or under-segmented by several baselines, while MultiStripUNet closely follows the ground truth for both. These visual results corroborate our quantitative advantage on small and thin organs.

\subsubsection{Computational Efficiency Comparison}

Table~\ref{table_efficiency} compares parameters, FLOPs, and peak inference GPU memory. MultiStripUNet achieves the lowest FLOPs (4.18G) and GPU memory (5.12\,GB) among all methods with only 26.21M parameters, about $4\times$ fewer parameters and $140\times$ fewer FLOPs than TransUNet \cite{chen2021transunet} (105.28M/593.00G), and less than VM-UNet \cite{ruan2024vmunet} (32.32M/5.27G) and Swin-UNet \cite{cao2021swinunet} (27.17M/5.95G). The strip-convolution design thus delivers competitive accuracy at a fraction of the cost of attention-based architectures.

\subsubsection{Strip vs. Square Kernels}

To directly verify the central claim that strip kernels are better suited to elongated anatomical structures than isotropic square kernels, we replace the strip branches in the MSC module with square depthwise kernels, keeping all other components unchanged. We compare three settings: a matched-parameter comparison at single scale ($6\times6$ square vs. $1\times17$+$17\times1$, which have comparable parameter counts), a matched-extent comparison ($17\times17$ square vs. $1\times17$+$17\times1$), and the multi-scale comparison at which the complete model operates ($[17\times17,\,21\times21]$ square vs. our final $[1\times17$+$17\times1,\;1\times21$+$21\times1]$). The results are reported in Table~\ref{table_strip_square}.

Three findings support the strip-over-square claim. First, at the multi-scale setting where the complete model operates, the strip pair outperforms its square counterpart by +1.81 mean DSC (85.86\% vs. 84.05\%), and the gains concentrate on the small target organs: Aorta +1.58, Gallbladder +4.56, and Pancreas +5.91. Second, the shape advantage holds for a single strip pair as well: although the $17\times17$ square kernel attains a marginally higher mean DSC than $1\times17$+$17\times1$ (83.96\% vs. 83.36\%), its scores on Aorta, Gallbladder, and Pancreas are substantially lower (86.39/68.12/66.43 vs. 88.02/72.35/73.94), suggesting that oversized square kernels dilute the fine details that small organs rely on. Third, when parameter budgets are matched, strip convolution is clearly superior: with comparable parameters (17.20M vs. 18.26M), the strip pair beats the $6\times6$ square kernel by +0.66 mean DSC, and by up to +9.68 on Pancreas (73.94 vs. 64.26). These gains also come at a far smaller parameter cost than square kernels of equal extent: 87\% fewer parameters than $17\times17$ (17.20M vs. 134.94M) and 95\% fewer than the multi-scale square pair (26.21M vs. 492.93M). Together, these results show that strip kernels consistently outperform square kernels on small, thin, and highly shape-variant organs, both at matched parameter budgets and at matched extents.

\section{Conclusions}

This paper has presented MultiStripUNet, an anatomy-aware medical image segmentation network built on the principle that the elongated geometry of anatomical structures should drive the design of the segmentation architecture. The Multi-Strip Convolution (MSC) module aligns strip kernels with the principal axes of anisotropic organs and is the core of the Multi-Strip Convolution Block (MSCB) constructing both the encoder and the decoder, while Multi-Strip Convolution Gates (MSCG) reweight the skip connections. 

With only 26.21M parameters, MultiStripUNet achieves 85.86\% mean DSC / 13.36\,mm HD95 on Synapse and 92.82\% mean DSC on ACDC, which is competitive with CNN-, Transformer-, and Mamba-based methods, and the largest gains fall on the small target organs. The ablations point to a clear design principle: for anisotropic anatomy, kernel shape matters more than kernel size, and strip kernels deliver their gains precisely on small and thin organs at a fraction of the parameter cost of square kernels. The spacing between strip scales, rather than their quantity, then determines the accuracy--efficiency trade-off.

Additionally, one limitation of this study is the 2D slice-wise evaluation; extending the strip design to 3D volumes, where anisotropy is even more pronounced, is a promising direction for future work.
